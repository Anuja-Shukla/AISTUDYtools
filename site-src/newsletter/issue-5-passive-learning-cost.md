Lecturing is efficient. One instructor can convey a framework, a set of definitions, and a worked example to a hundred students in fifty minutes. It's hard to beat for pure information transfer, and there's a reason it's remained the default format in business education for so long.

But information transfer and judgment-building are not the same thing, and business programs are ultimately trying to produce the second, not just the first.

## What passive learning actually leaves out

A student who has heard a lecture on positioning strategy can usually define positioning, list a few examples, and recognize it on an exam question. What they often can't do yet is *use* it — recognize an ambiguous, messy real-world situation as a positioning problem and reason their way to a defensible answer under pressure.

That gap between knowing a concept and being able to apply it under real constraints is where a lot of classroom learning quietly loses its value. Students graduate able to recite frameworks they've never actually had to use to solve anything.

## Why "I understood it in class" is a weak signal

Ask any student whether a lecture made sense while they were sitting in it, and most will say yes. Comprehension in the moment is a poor predictor of whether that knowledge will surface later, unprompted, when it's actually needed. The moment a framework proves useful is rarely the moment it was first explained — it's usually much later, in a context that doesn't announce itself as "the Porter's Five Forces situation."

Active application closes that gap by forcing the retrieval to happen earlier and under more realistic conditions. A student who has had to *decide* using a framework, and defend that decision to a skeptical audience, tends to retain not just the framework but the judgment about when and how to reach for it.

## Practical ways to shift the balance

Shifting away from passive formats doesn't require abandoning lectures — it means treating them as the setup, not the whole lesson.

- **Follow a concept lecture with a decision, not just a recap question.** Instead of "summarize the four Ps," give students a scenario and ask which lever they'd pull first, and why.
- **Build in a moment where students commit to an answer before seeing more information.** Committing under uncertainty, then updating, mirrors how real decisions get made far more than reading a complete case and then answering a question.
- **Make the stakes feel real, even in a simulated exercise.** A simulation with a visible scorecard, a competitor reacting to choices, or a consequence that plays out over multiple rounds creates the kind of feedback loop that a static case study can't.

## The trade-off worth making

None of this means lectures are obsolete — they remain the fastest way to introduce a concept to a room. The shift worth making is treating the lecture as step one of two, not the whole exercise. The second step, where students actually have to use what they just heard to make a call they'll be challenged on, is where the concept moves from "recognized" to "usable" — and that distinction is the entire point of a business education.
