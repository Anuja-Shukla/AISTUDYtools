# Lesson 3.7 — Index Numbers
Welcome to the last lesson: index numbers. They measure how prices, quantities or values change over time.
An index number compares the current period with a base period, which is set to 100. An index of 123 means a rise of 23% since the base. The Consumer Price Index and the Wholesale Price Index are everyday examples. || An index number compares the current period with a base period, which is set to a hundred. An index of one twenty-three means a rise of twenty-three percent since the base. The Consumer Price Index and the Wholesale Price Index are everyday examples.

The simplest index adds up the prices: Σp₁ ÷ Σp₀ × 100 = 245 ÷ 200 × 100 = 122.5. But it ignores how much of each item is bought. || The simplest index adds up the prices. The current total, two forty-five, over the base total, two hundred, times a hundred, gives one twenty-two point five. But it ignores how much of each item is bought.
Laspeyres weights prices by base-year quantities: Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × 100 = 1,430 ÷ 1,160 × 100 ≈ 123.28. || Laspeyres weights prices by base-year quantities. The sum of p one q zero, over the sum of p zero q zero, times a hundred. That is one thousand four hundred and thirty over one thousand one hundred and sixty, about one twenty-three point two eight.
Paasche uses current-year quantities instead: Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁ × 100 = 1,375 ÷ 1,120 × 100 ≈ 122.77. || Paasche uses current-year quantities instead. The sum of p one q one, over the sum of p zero q one, times a hundred. That is one thousand three hundred and seventy-five over one thousand one hundred and twenty, about one twenty-two point seven seven.
Fisher's ideal index is the geometric mean of the two: √(123.28 × 122.77) ≈ 123.02. Bowley's index is their arithmetic mean, and Marshall–Edgeworth uses q₀ + q₁ as weights. || Fisher's ideal index is the geometric mean of the two. The square root of one twenty-three point two eight times one twenty-two point seven seven, about one twenty-three point zero two. Bowley's index is their arithmetic mean, and Marshall-Edgeworth uses the sum of both quantities as weights.
Why “ideal”? A good index should pass the time reversal test, P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 1, and the factor reversal test, price index × quantity index = value index. Fisher's index passes both; Laspeyres and Paasche pass neither. || Why ideal? A good index should pass the time reversal test, where the forward index times the backward index equals one. And the factor reversal test, where the price index times the quantity index equals the value index. Fisher's index passes both. Laspeyres and Paasche pass neither.
To shift the base, divide every index by the new base year's index and multiply by 100. If 2024 is 125 and 2025 is 150, then with 2024 as the base, 2025 becomes 150 ÷ 125 × 100 = 120. || To shift the base, divide every index by the new base year's index, and multiply by a hundred. If twenty twenty-four is a hundred and twenty-five, and twenty twenty-five is a hundred and fifty, then with twenty twenty-four as the base, twenty twenty-five becomes a hundred and twenty.
Deflating turns money values into real values: real value = money value ÷ price index × 100. A salary of ₹60,000 when the CPI is 150 is worth ₹40,000 at base-year prices. || Deflating turns money values into real values. Real value is the money value, divided by the price index, times a hundred. A salary of sixty thousand rupees, when the C P I is a hundred and fifty, is worth forty thousand rupees at base-year prices.
Index numbers are used to measure inflation, to adjust wages and dearness allowance, and to compare real growth over the years.
