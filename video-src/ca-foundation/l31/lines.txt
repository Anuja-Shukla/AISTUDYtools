# Lesson 3.1 — Statistical Description of Data and Sampling
Welcome! In this lesson we describe data: how it is collected, classified and grouped, how it is charted, and how samples are chosen.
Data is either primary or secondary. Primary data is collected first-hand for your study. Secondary data was collected by someone else, such as a government report.
We classify data in four ways: by time, which is chronological; by place, which is geographical; by attribute, which is qualitative; and by measured value, which is quantitative.

Add the frequencies as you go to get the cumulative frequencies: 1, 3, 10, 17 and 20. So 10 students scored less than 60. || Add the frequencies as you go to get the cumulative frequencies: one, three, ten, seventeen and twenty. So ten students scored less than sixty.
Choose the right chart. Bar and pie charts suit categories. A histogram suits continuous classes: its bars touch, and their areas show the frequencies.
A frequency polygon joins the mid-points of the bars. An ogive plots the cumulative frequencies, and the less-than and more-than ogives cross at the median.
A census studies every member of the population. A sample studies part of it, saving time and money. A value describing the population is a parameter; one describing a sample is a statistic.
Random sampling gives every unit a known chance: simple random, stratified, where you sample from each group, systematic, and cluster sampling.
For a systematic sample of 50 from a list of 1,000, the interval is 1,000 ÷ 50 = 20. Pick a random start from 1 to 20, then take every 20th name. || For a systematic sample of fifty from a list of one thousand, the interval is one thousand divided by fifty, which is twenty. Pick a random start from one to twenty, then take every twentieth name.
Purposive, quota and convenience samples are not random. And remember two kinds of error: sampling errors come from studying only part of the population; non-sampling errors, such as wrong answers or recording mistakes, can happen even in a census.
