# Lesson 3.4 — Probability
Welcome! This lesson is about probability: the language of chance, the addition and multiplication theorems, and expected value.
A random experiment has outcomes we can't predict for certain. All the possible outcomes form the sample space. A coin has 2, a die has 6, two dice have 36, and a pack of cards has 52. || A random experiment has outcomes we can't predict for certain. All the possible outcomes form the sample space. A coin has two, a die has six, two dice have thirty-six, and a pack of cards has fifty-two.
When outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total. It always lies between 0 and 1, and P(not A) = 1 − P(A). || When outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes, divided by the total. It always lies between zero and one. And the probability of not A is one minus the probability of A.
Throw two dice. Six of the 36 outcomes add up to 7, so P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. Seven is the most likely total. || Throw two dice. Six of the thirty-six outcomes add up to seven. So the probability of a sum of seven is six over thirty-six, or one sixth. Seven is the most likely total.
Odds are another way to state a chance. If the odds in favour are a to b, the probability is a ÷ (a + b). Odds of 3 to 5 in favour give 3/8. || Odds are another way to state a chance. If the odds in favour are a to b, the probability is a over a plus b. Odds of three to five in favour give three eighths.
Don't confuse two ideas. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together. Independent events can happen together, but one does not change the chance of the other.
For “or”, use the addition theorem: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). P(king or heart) = 4/52 + 13/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13. || For “or”, use the addition theorem. The probability of A or B is P of A, plus P of B, minus P of both. The probability of a king or a heart is four, plus thirteen, minus one, all over fifty-two. That is sixteen over fifty-two, or four thirteenths.
For “and”, use the multiplication theorem: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A). Two aces in a row without replacement: 4/52 × 3/51 = 1/221. || For “and”, use the multiplication theorem. The probability of A and B is P of A, times P of B given A. Two aces in a row, without replacement, is four over fifty-two, times three over fifty-one. That is one over two hundred and twenty-one.
When A and B are independent, simply multiply: if P(A) = 0.5 and P(B) = 0.4, then P(A and B) = 0.2. || When A and B are independent, simply multiply. If P of A is zero point five, and P of B is zero point four, then P of A and B is zero point two.
Expected value is the long-run average: E(X) = Σx·p. A game pays ₹100 with probability 0.2, ₹20 with 0.5 and nothing with 0.3. E(X) = 20 + 10 + 0 = ₹30. || Expected value is the long-run average. Multiply each value by its probability, and add. A game pays a hundred rupees with probability zero point two, twenty rupees with zero point five, and nothing with zero point three. The expected value is twenty plus ten plus zero, which is thirty rupees.
If the game costs ₹35 to play, a player loses ₹5 a game on average. The variance is E(X²) − [E(X)]² = 2,200 − 900 = 1,300. || If the game costs thirty-five rupees to play, a player loses five rupees a game on average. The variance is E of X squared, minus E of X, squared. That is two thousand two hundred, minus nine hundred, which is one thousand three hundred.
Try this: P(A) = 0.6, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.3. Then P(A ∪ B) = 0.6 + 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.8. || Try this. P of A is zero point six, P of B is zero point five, and P of both is zero point three. What is P of A or B? Zero point six plus zero point five, minus zero point three, is zero point eight. The answer is A.
